The short answer is: you can get into real trouble if you use the wrong words.
Here's the distinction that matters legally:
What you ARE:
A licensed drone pilot providing aerial photography and video documentation of rooftop conditions. That's it. That's the whole product. You're selling eyes in the sky and footage. You are not evaluating structural integrity, you are not providing a professional assessment, and you are not signing off on anything.
What you are NOT:
A building inspector, structural inspector, roofing inspector, or professional engineer. In Ontario, those titles carry regulatory weight. Calling yourself an "inspector" when you're charging money for assessments could potentially bump up against the Professional Engineers Act or Ontario's Building Code Act depending on how far you take the claims. You don't need a license to fly a drone and hand someone footage — but if you start writing reports that say "this roof is structurally compromised" or "this building is unsafe," you've crossed into professional opinion territory that you're not licensed to give.
The practical line:

✅ "I provide aerial drone footage of your roof so you can see its condition"
✅ "Northern Drone Aerial Documentation" or "Northern Drone Roof Surveys"
✅ "Here's what your roof looks like from above after the rainfall"
✅ "You can see pooling water in this area — you may want to have a roofer take a look"
❌ "I inspected your roof and found damage"
❌ "Based on my inspection, this roof needs repair"
❌ Calling yourself a "drone roof inspector" on marketing materials

The word "inspection" isn't universally banned for non-professionals — home buyers hire non-licensed people to walk through properties all the time — but in a commercial context where someone might rely on your "inspection" to make a financial decision, and something goes wrong, you want no part of that liability chain.
